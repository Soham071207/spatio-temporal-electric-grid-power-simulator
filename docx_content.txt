1. Executive Summary
The proposed research investigates whether a graph-based spatio-temporal model can improve short-term electricity forecasting for Spain by learning relationships among geographically or electrically related regions rather than treating the country as a single isolated time series. The central hypothesis is that electricity demand and renewable generation are not purely temporal signals: regional weather, calendar effects, generation mix, interconnection structure, and neighboring demand patterns create dependencies that can be represented as a graph.
The project is deliberately positioned beyond a simple 'apply GNN to Spain' study. Recent literature already contains strong spatio-temporal GNN methods for load forecasting, while a 2026 study specifically evaluates hybrid deep learning for Spanish electricity load and price forecasting. Therefore, the defensible contribution must come from the combination of a Spain-specific multi-node formulation, physically informed graph construction, dynamic/adaptive relationships, exogenous weather and renewable features, rigorous leakage-safe evaluation, and strong ablations—not from merely using a GNN.
Red Eléctrica (REE) reports that Spain's 2025 electricity demand reached 256,086 GWh, up 2.8% year-on-year, while renewables supplied 55.5% of generation (56.6% including estimated self-consumption). REE also reports a 40,070 MW peninsular peak on 15 January 2025. These characteristics make forecasting increasingly relevant as demand grows and renewable penetration rises. citeturn2search1
Recommended primary task: day-ahead hourly forecasting of regional electricity demand, with a secondary multi-output extension for renewable generation and/or net load. The recommended model family is an ST-GNN with temporal convolution/attention plus graph convolution and an adaptive graph component. STGCN, DCRNN, Graph WaveNet, MTGNN, and recent energy-specific GNNs should form the methodological lineage and baseline set. citeturn3academia38turn3academia36turn3academia39turn3academia37
2. Research Problem and Motivation
2.1 Core problem
Given historical hourly observations X(t-L+1:t) for multiple Spanish energy nodes, estimate the future load y(t+1:t+H), where L is the historical look-back window and H is the forecast horizon. The model should use temporal structure, inter-node dependencies, weather, calendar variables, and relevant generation-system information.
A single national time series can be forecast with ARIMA, gradient boosting, LSTM, TCN, Transformer, or other sequence models. However, a national aggregate removes spatial information. A graph formulation preserves multiple regional signals and allows information to propagate between nodes.
2.2 Why Spain is a strong case study
REE describes the Spanish system as containing multiple electrical systems; the peninsular system accounts for about 94% of national demand and is connected to France, Portugal, Andorra and Morocco, while the Balearic and Canary systems have distinct operational characteristics. citeturn2search8
Spain's renewable penetration is high and evolving rapidly. In 2025 renewables supplied 55.5% of national generation, rising to 56.6% when estimated self-consumption is included. citeturn2search1
Demand and generation conditions are changing: REE reports 2025 installed generation capacity of 142.5 GW and nearly 10 GW of new wind and solar PV capacity during the year. citeturn2search1
REE's REData API provides GET-based access to backend data used by its REData application, including filtering by date range, time aggregation and geographic identifiers. citeturn2search12
The project therefore has practical relevance to grid operations, renewable integration, storage scheduling, demand response and energy-market planning.
3. Research Objectives and Questions
3.1 Objectives
Build a reproducible Spain-specific multi-node energy forecasting dataset.
Construct a graph using physical/geographical/electrical relationships and test whether adaptive relationships improve forecasting.
Develop an ST-GNN that jointly learns temporal dynamics and spatial dependencies.
Benchmark against statistical, machine-learning, sequence-deep-learning and GNN baselines.
Quantify the contribution of graph structure, weather, renewable features and adaptive adjacency through ablation studies.
Evaluate generalization across seasons, extreme-demand periods, renewable regimes and regional nodes.
Produce a publication-ready experimental protocol with reproducible code, data provenance and model configuration.

6. Research Gap and Novelty Positioning
6.1 Gap
The most defensible gap is not 'GNNs have not been used for energy forecasting.' They have. Instead, the gap is the lack of a carefully validated Spain-specific regional graph forecasting framework that explicitly compares physically informed, geographic, statistical and learned graph structures while integrating exogenous weather and renewable-system variables under a leakage-safe temporal evaluation.
6.2 Proposed novelty
Spain-specific regional graph representation for electricity demand forecasting.
Multi-view graph construction: geographic proximity + electrical-system relationships + data-driven similarity.
Hybrid fixed/adaptive adjacency so the model can respect physical structure while discovering latent dependencies.
Joint temporal modeling and exogenous drivers (weather/calendar/renewable context).
Regime-aware evaluation covering normal, peak-demand and high-renewable conditions.
Graph ablation that tests whether learned relationships are genuinely useful rather than decorative.
Reproducible open-data pipeline using authoritative REE/REData sources where permitted.
6.3 Novelty claim wording to use later
Suggested paper claim: 'We propose a Spain-specific spatio-temporal graph learning framework for short-term regional electricity demand forecasting that combines physically informed and adaptive graph structures with meteorological and power-system covariates, and systematically evaluates the contribution of each information source under temporally ordered and regime-stratified experiments.'
Do not claim 'first GNN for Spain' unless a final systematic literature search proves it. Current evidence already includes Spain-specific deep-learning load forecasting, so a broad first-of-kind claim would be unsafe. citeturn0search3
7. Data Sources and Acquisition Plan
7.1 Primary electricity source — REE
Red Eléctrica's REData ecosystem is the preferred authoritative source. Its API is a REST service using GET requests and exposes backend data used by REData widgets; the documented request structure supports category/widget selection, date ranges, time aggregation and geographic identifiers. citeturn2search12
REE also publishes annual system reports with complementary Excel series for demand, generation, international exchanges and other system indicators, which can be used for validation and historical cross-checking. citeturn2search0turn2search5
7.2 Secondary electricity source — ENTSO-E
ENTSO-E Transparency Platform data should be used as a cross-check and, where licensing/access permits, as a secondary source for actual total load, generation, forecasts and cross-border exchanges. The final implementation should record the exact dataset identifier and extraction timestamp.
7.3 Weather
Use hourly weather observations/reanalysis for the selected regions. Candidate variables: temperature, apparent temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, wind speed, wind direction, surface solar radiation/cloud cover and pressure. Weather must be aligned to forecast availability: when predicting t+1:t+24, only weather information that would realistically have been available at prediction time should be used. If forecast weather is unavailable, do not accidentally use future observations.
7.4 Recommended initial data window
Use the longest common high-quality hourly period available across all selected regional targets and covariates. A practical first version can target approximately 2018–2025 for model development, with 2026 held as a strict forward test if complete data are available. The exact period must be determined after checking data continuity, definitions and geographic coverage.
7.5 Data acquisition checklist
Download/query REE hourly demand for the selected geographic level.
Download/query generation by technology if T3/T4 are included.
Download weather for each node or representative weather station/grid cell.
Download calendar information: hour, weekday, month, holiday, working-day indicator.
Record source URL/API endpoint, extraction timestamp, timezone, units and version.
Save raw data unchanged before cleaning.
Create a data dictionary and missingness report.

8.2 Target formulation
For node i, input tensor X_i ∈ R^(L×F) contains L historical time steps and F features. The target Y_i contains H future demand values. The full batch can be represented as X ∈ R^(B×L×N×F), where N is the number of nodes.
8.3 Leakage controls
Fit scalers only on training data.
Never randomly shuffle time-series observations across train/validation/test.
Do not use future realized weather when the model is intended to represent operational day-ahead forecasting.
Construct lag and rolling features using past-only windows.
When building correlation-based graphs, estimate correlations using the training period only, then freeze them for validation/test unless the research explicitly studies online graph updates.
Do not tune hyperparameters on the final test period.

9.2 Multi-view adjacency
Define three adjacency matrices: A_geo for geographic proximity, A_elec for electrical/interconnection structure, and A_data for historical similarity. A learned adjacency A_adapt can then be optimized by the model. A combined graph can be expressed as A = αA_geo + βA_elec + γA_data + δA_adapt, with normalized weights or learnable gates.
For A_geo, a distance kernel can be used: A_ij = exp(-d_ij²/σ²) for selected neighbors. For A_data, similarity can use training-only correlation or mutual-information-like measures. For A_elec, use documented interconnections where available. The paper should test each graph separately and in combination.
9.3 Critical scientific test
If a purely geographic graph performs no better than a temporal-only baseline, that is still informative. It may indicate that the selected regional resolution is too coarse, that demand is dominated by common national factors, or that graph edges do not encode useful predictive dependencies. The study should report this rather than forcing a positive result.
10. Proposed Model Architecture
Recommended architecture: Multi-View Adaptive Spatio-Temporal GNN (MV-ASTGNN). The name is a working label, not a final publication title.
Block
Function
Input encoder
Normalize and embed node features.
Temporal module
Dilated temporal convolution and/or temporal self-attention for hourly, daily and weekly patterns.
Spatial module
Graph convolution/message passing over A_geo, A_elec and/or A_data.
Adaptive graph learner
Learn latent dependencies using node embeddings, as inspired by Graph WaveNet/MTGNN.
Fusion/gating
Combine physical and learned graph representations.
Multi-horizon decoder
Directly output H future time points to reduce recursive error accumulation.
Output heads
Demand head; optional renewable/net-load heads.
10.1 Conceptual flow
Historical regional signals + weather + calendar + renewable context → temporal embedding → physical graph message passing → adaptive graph message passing → spatio-temporal fusion/attention → multi-horizon decoder → 1–24 h regional forecasts → aggregate regional predictions to national forecast.
10.2 Loss function
Start with weighted MAE or Huber loss. A multi-objective formulation can be tested later: L = λ1·MAE + λ2·RMSE + λ3·temporal-consistency/ramp loss. Avoid overly complicated loss functions in the first version; isolate each contribution experimentally.
10.3 Probabilistic extension
For a later version, predict quantiles q∈{0.1,0.5,0.9} using pinball loss. This would make the model more useful for operational uncertainty management and strengthen the paper if implemented correctly.

12.2 Time split
Use chronological splits. Example: train = earliest 70%, validation = next 15%, test = final 15%, or a calendar split such as 2018–2023 train, 2024 validation, 2025 test, with 2026 reserved for an out-of-time robustness evaluation if data completeness permits. The exact split must be chosen after inspecting the available data and then frozen.
12.3 Statistical significance
Repeat training with multiple random seeds. Report mean ± standard deviation. For paired forecast errors across identical timestamps, use an appropriate paired statistical test or Diebold–Mariano-style forecast comparison where assumptions are satisfied. Include effect size, not only p-values.

14. Explainability and Error Analysis
Visualize learned adjacency/edge weights and compare them with physical distance and known system connectivity.
Plot attention/importance over hours to identify daily and weekly dependencies.
Use feature perturbation or SHAP-like analysis for weather/calendar/system variables where computationally feasible.
Break errors down by region, month, hour-of-day, weekday/weekend, temperature regime, and demand percentile.
Analyze the worst 20 forecast timestamps and classify causes: weather shock, holiday, peak ramp, renewable variability, missing data, or structural change.
Interpretability should be treated as evidence about model behavior, not as proof of causality. A high learned edge weight means predictive utility under the model, not a physical causal relationship.

18. Publication Strategy
18.1 Publication potential
The project has a credible publication path, but the current topic is competitive. Recent papers in 2024–2026 already demonstrate adaptive spatio-temporal GNNs, multi-area load forecasting, graph-based feeder forecasting, multi-resolution GNNs, and Spain-specific deep-learning forecasting. citeturn0search0turn0search1turn0search2turn0search6turn0search3
The strongest publication angle is therefore an experimentally rigorous, Spain-specific graph-learning benchmark and model rather than a generic architecture paper.
18.2 Suggested paper structure
1. Introduction — motivation, Spain energy transition, forecasting challenge, contributions.
2. Related Work — load forecasting, ST-GNNs, energy GNNs, Spain studies.
3. Dataset and Problem Formulation — nodes, targets, features, graph definitions.
4. Proposed Method — physical/adaptive graph, temporal module, decoder, loss.
5. Experimental Setup — splits, baselines, metrics, hyperparameters.
6. Results — main table, regional results, horizon results.
7. Ablation — graph/weather/renewable/node-resolution studies.
8. Robustness and Explainability — peaks, high renewable periods, learned graph.
9. Limitations — data granularity, transferability, forecast-weather assumptions.
10. Conclusion — concise findings and future work.

19.1 Minimum viable research milestone
Do not begin with a very complex architecture. First prove: (1) clean regional dataset, (2) strong LSTM/TCN baseline, (3) simple STGCN, (4) graph ablation, and (5) statistically defensible improvement. Only then add adaptive graph learning, attention, multi-resolution blocks or probabilistic forecasting.
